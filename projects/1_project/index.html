Decaying Turbulence via DNS

The asymptotic state of decaying homogeneous turbulence, resolved with record-duration direct numerical simulations.

How does turbulence die out once nothing is stirring it? If the kinetic energy decays as a power law, \(E_n \sim t^{-n}\), classical theory gives two answers that depend on the largest scales of the initial flow. A Birkhoff–Saffman (BS) spectrum, \(E(k) \sim k^2\) at small \(k\), gives \(n = 6/5\). A Loitsianskii–Kolmogorov–Batchelor (LKB) spectrum, \(E(k) \sim k^4\), gives \(n = 10/7\). Experiments and earlier simulations report anything from \(n \approx 1.15\) to \(1.45\). They are limited by short run times, initial spectra that aren’t controlled, and the finite size of the domain.

My PhD research, with Prof. K. R. Sreenivasan at NYU, settles this with direct numerical simulations (DNS) that are long enough, large enough and controlled enough to reach the asymptotic state. It also uses the results to test Alexander Migdal’s recent field-theoretic solution of decaying turbulence.

A velocity component on a plane through a 256³ decaying-turbulence DNS. With no forcing, the small eddies die first and the remaining structures grow larger.

Simulations

  • Solver: a pseudo-spectral Navier–Stokes solver in a triply periodic box, with phase-shift de-aliasing and semi-implicit time stepping.
  • Controlled initial conditions: random Gaussian fields with a model spectrum whose low-wavenumber slope is set exactly (\(k^2\) for BS, \(k^4\) for LKB). The initial integral scale is about 1% of the box, and 0.54–0.58% for the longest runs, to delay confinement effects.
  • Scale: initial \(Re_\lambda\) from 30 to 145 on grids from \(512^3\) to \(4096^3\), with 3–10 independent realizations per case.
  • Duration: up to about 200,000 initial eddy-turnover times. Earlier studies ran for around 1,000.
  • Systems: TACC (through an ACCESS allocation), NYU’s HPC cluster and KAUST’s Shaheen III.

Dynamic regridding

As turbulence decays, the smallest (Kolmogorov) scale grows and a fixed grid becomes wastefully over-resolved. I designed a regridding scheme that halves the grid whenever the resolution parameter \(k_{max}\eta\) exceeds 6, which resets it to about 3, still double the usual DNS requirement. Each step removes 7/8 of the degrees of freedom and roughly doubles the time step, which is about 16× cheaper per unit of simulated time. Over a full run this cut the cost by more than 5×. A sensitivity study showed that more aggressive thresholds distort the decay rate, while \(k_{max}\eta = 6\) is indistinguishable from the run without regridding.

Validating the regridding scheme at \(Re_\lambda = 93\) on a \(2048^3\) grid: the local decay exponent \(n(t)\) with and without grid modification. Lower thresholds drift away from the reference; \(k_{max}\eta = 6\) overlaps it exactly.

Key findings

Clean power laws with exponents that depend on the initial condition. After a short transient, both families decay as clean power laws. BS runs settle at \(n \approx 1.25\) at the highest Reynolds numbers. LKB runs hold Kolmogorov’s \(n = 10/7\) for more than 2,000 turnover times. The decay exponent depends on the large-scale structure of the initial flow, so it is not universal.

Kinetic energy decay for BS (left, reference slope −5/4) and LKB (right, reference slope −10/7) initial spectra, across initial Reynolds numbers.

The finite box sets the limit. In every case the power law breaks down when the integral length grows to about 10–15% of the box. A smaller initial integral scale lengthens the self-similar decay; that is why the long runs started with such small energetic scales.

How the spectrum evolves. The low-wavenumber \(k^2\) range of the BS case survives throughout the decay, as the “permanence of large eddies” predicts. The initial \(k^{-5/3}\) range disappears within a few turnover times and is replaced by a \(k^{-1}\) range. Scaled by the energy and the integral length, spectra from all times collapse onto one curve.

Energy spectra for a BS run at \(Re_\lambda = 105\): raw spectra over time (left) and in self-similar coordinates \(E(k)/(E_n L)\) against \(kL\) (right).

A test of Migdal’s theory. For the BS case the agreement is excellent. The Migdal length grows as \(t^{0.53}\) against a predicted \(t^{1/2}\), the energy–length relation follows the predicted slope, and the scaling exponent of the second-order structure function collapses onto the theoretical curve. For the LKB case the energy decay departs from the predicted \(n = 5/4\), but the structure-function shape still matches the theory. The predicted \(k^{-7/2}\) high-wavenumber tail is not visible at these Reynolds numbers.

Local scaling exponent of the second-order structure function, \(\zeta_2\), against \(\log(r/L_M)\) for the BS run at \(Re_\lambda = 145\): at several times (left) and time-averaged with its spread (right), compared with Migdal's prediction.

Where universality may lie. Removing the lowest few wavenumbers changes the “bulk” energy decay noticeably, but barely changes the enstrophy decay. Energy decay is shaped by these large-scale boundary effects, so universality is more likely to show up in the enstrophy.

Ongoing work

  • Non-equilibrium energy flux (manuscript in preparation). In decaying turbulence the energy flux through the inertial range is not constant. I show that, normalized by the instantaneous dissipation, it collapses onto a single curve that grows with \(kL\). A self-similar analysis predicts it with no free parameters. This explains why the inertial-range spectrum is shallower than \(-5/3\).
  • A multi-GPU solver for \(8192^3\). I’m writing a CUDA pseudo-spectral solver to push these simulations to higher Reynolds numbers. Progress and benchmarks are on my SC’26 page.
  • ML surrogate models (planned). The goal is data-driven surrogates that forecast how the energy spectrum evolves and generate velocity fields consistent with it, trained and validated on DNS ensembles.
