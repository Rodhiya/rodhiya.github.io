Vapor Chamber for Fiber-Laser Cooling

A vapor-chamber and heat-sink assembly to air-cool a high-power fiber laser.

High-power fiber lasers concentrate their waste heat in a few small, hot spots. In a summer internship (May–July 2018) at a defense research lab, I designed an air-cooled assembly to remove 800 W from two heat sources, using only a 450 × 250 × 120 mm space.

The design spreads each source’s heat with a two-phase vapor chamber, so a shared pin-fin heat sink can reject it to forced air at a modest air speed.

Left: my design in cross-section (not to scale). Two water-filled copper vapor chambers spread the heat from each source into a shared aluminum pin-fin array cooled by forced air. Right: the assembly envelope, with the vapor chambers (blue) above and below the fins.

How a vapor chamber works

A vapor chamber is a flat heat pipe. Under the heat source, the working fluid (water) evaporates. The vapor spreads across the chamber and condenses on the cooler face, giving up its latent heat, and a porous wick pumps the liquid back by capillary action. The vapor space stays almost isothermal, so the chamber behaves like a plate with a conductivity well above solid copper’s.

Choosing the wick

The wick has to pump liquid across the full 250 mm width of the chamber. It works only if its capillary pressure (\(2\sigma/r\)) exceeds the viscous pressure drop of the liquid flowing through it, which I estimated with Darcy’s law integrated over the chamber, accounting for evaporation and condensation along the path. Fine pores raise the capillary pressure but also the flow resistance, so the choice is a real trade-off:

Wick (copper) Pore radius Permeability Porosity Capillary pressure Liquid pressure loss Verdict
Sintered powder 0.009 mm 1.7 × 10⁻¹² m² 52% 15,100 Pa 44,500 Pa fails
Foam 0.21 mm 1.9 × 10⁻⁹ m² 91% 647 Pa 41 Pa works

The final chamber has a 1 mm copper wall, a 0.5 mm copper-foam wick and a 2 mm vapor space. Treating the wall and wick as series thermal resistances, with the vapor phase nearly isothermal, gives an effective conductivity of about 637 W/m·K with the foam wick (458 W/m·K with sintered powder), against 401 W/m·K for solid copper.

Sizing the heat sink

I compared two heat-sink families within the same envelope:

  • Rectangular fins: spaced at twice the thermal boundary-layer thickness at the fin’s trailing edge, so neighbouring boundary layers don’t merge, with the fin thickness chosen to maximise fin effectiveness.
  • Staggered pin fins: heat transfer from tube-bank correlations for flow across pin arrays, with row-count corrections and adiabatic-tip fin efficiency. I wrote a small C++ program to sweep pin diameter and air speed.

Even the best rectangular fins (2 mm thick) reach only about 785 W at 10 m/s. Pin-fin arrays pass the 800 W target at 2.5–5.5 m/s, depending on pin diameter. The final design uses staggered aluminum pins with a transverse pitch of 2.5 diameters, which reject about 870 W at 3.5 m/s with 15 mm pins.

Materials

I also surveyed candidate materials for the assembly and its optical mounts, comparing thermal conductivity, expansion, diffusivity, strength and machinability. The candidates were copper, aluminum, titanium and magnesium, plus the low-expansion Zerodur glass-ceramic and Invar (FeNi36).
